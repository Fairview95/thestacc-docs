Your keyword pool supplies topics for theStacc's content planning. Choose phrases that describe questions your business can answer, then review the resulting plan before generating articles. A saved keyword does not guarantee a particular publication date, search position or amount of traffic.

Open **Content SEO > Settings > Keywords** to manage the pool. Adding, renaming and other changes can require project editor access; some actions also require an active Content SEO subscription.

## What a good keyword looks like

A useful keyword connects a reader's task with something your business actually offers or can explain. Start with the service, problem or decision, then add an audience or location when it changes the answer.

For example, *bookkeeping for Shopify sellers* describes a different task from *bookkeeping*. The modifier helps define the article; it does not establish search demand or make the phrase easy to rank for. Check your customer questions and available search evidence before adding it.

Ask three questions:

1. What would the reader need to know or do after finding this page?
2. Can our business provide an accurate, useful answer?
3. Does an existing page already answer the same question?

### Patterns that tend to work

These are illustrative ways to make a topic more specific:

- **Service and audience:** bookkeeping for Shopify sellers.
- **Task and constraint:** choosing a CRM for a two-person sales team.
- **Comparison and use case:** Stripe vs Square for salon payments.
- **Service and place:** emergency AC repair in Phoenix.

Use a place only when your business serves it. Use a year only when the information needs a dated comparison or update.

## Keyword shapes to avoid

Rewrite phrases that do not explain a reader's task, such as *next-generation growth solutions*. Avoid strings of repeated modifiers, such as *best top cheap CRM software tool*.

A short keyword can still describe a legitimate topic. Length alone does not establish whether a topic is worth covering. The planning prompts encourage specific, business-relevant phrases, but you should still inspect suggested keywords rather than assuming the AI rejects every weak idea.

When adding keywords, keep each phrase within **80 characters**. The current input parser omits empty or overlong entries, so review what was actually added instead of assuming every pasted line was saved.

## Targeting more than one location

Keep your service areas accurate under **Content SEO > Settings > Business Details**. See [Business Info](/content-seo/business-setup).

The planning prompts ask for coverage across listed locations. For three or more locations, they include instructions about non-primary locations and geographic variety. These are generation instructions, rather than a guarantee that every returned calendar meets a fixed distribution.

Inspect the plan for places you actually serve, missing important areas and unnecessary city variations. Separate pages should provide useful differences for readers; changing a city name alone does not create a distinct topic.

## How keywords are stored: normalization and no duplicates

The storage normalizer lowercases keywords and trims spaces at the beginning and end. *Germany* and *germany* therefore produce the same stored text. Existing matching text is reported as a duplicate when you add keywords.

This check compares normalized text. It does not prove that two differently worded keywords serve different search intents, or that a topic can never appear again in a future plan. Review related phrases against your existing articles.

The pool has capacity checks. If adding keywords is blocked by an active-keyword or pool limit, read the returned message before retrying. Disabling a keyword and removing it are different actions; disabling does not necessarily free capacity for stored entries.

## The rotation pool: how theStacc picks each day's keyword

The rotation engine first checks whether a keyword is eligible. It must be enabled, free from an upcoming or in-progress article that occupies it, and outside its cooldown unless it is Always-On.

Among eligible keywords, selection prefers:

1. Always-On keywords.
2. Keywords that have not been used.
3. Keywords with the oldest recorded use.
4. The earliest-added keyword when the earlier criteria tie.

Always-On changes priority and cooldown eligibility. It does not let a keyword bypass an article already occupying it. Planning can also check topic overlap before accepting a selected phrase, so the selection order is not a promise that a particular keyword will appear next.

### When the pool runs dry

If no eligible keyword can be claimed, planning can fall back to AI-suggested topics. That fallback can also occur when selection fails. It does not guarantee that a calendar gap will be filled successfully.

Check disabled, cooling and active keywords before adding more. Then review any suggested topic for business relevance and overlap with published content. See [Content Plans & Keywords](/content-seo/content-plans).

## Cooldown: resting a keyword before reusing it

The default cooldown in the current configuration is **60 days**. Keyword use is recorded when generated content consumes generation allowance; it is not tied only to successful publication. A keyword can therefore start resting while its article still needs review.

After the configured cooldown, an otherwise eligible keyword can return to rotation. Eligibility does not guarantee immediate selection. Check the keyword's status and recorded use rather than counting from the day an article went live.

### Always-On keywords skip the cooldown

Star a keyword to mark it **Always-On**. It becomes exempt from the resting period and receives selection priority, while the active-article exclusion still applies.

Use this for topics that need continued attention. If an existing article already answers the question, improving that article may be more useful than commissioning another with the same intent.

## Best practice: don't over-repeat a keyword

Review the purpose of a new article before repeating a keyword. A pricing page, setup guide and comparison can serve different tasks; several pages giving the same answer can confuse navigation and content planning.

There is no universal Google rule allowing exactly two or three articles per keyword each month. Choose an update or a distinct new page based on the reader's task and your existing inventory, rather than treating a monthly count as a safe ranking threshold.

## Managing your pool

Paste keywords one per line or separated by commas, then select **Add to pool**. Check the response for added entries, duplicates, failures or entries left in the pool. An accepted addition does not prove that an article has been generated.

**Generate keywords** requests an AI-suggested batch after your website has been analyzed. Current configuration defaults allow five generation requests per project per UTC calendar month and request 12 candidates per batch. Duplicate filtering or unsuccessful saves can leave fewer than 12 new keywords. Check the displayed number added and remaining requests; deployed settings and availability can differ.

Search by text or filter by status and Always-On. Turn a keyword off to exclude it from selection, or star it to change priority. Review the article calendar after changes so the pool and planned content make sense together.

### Bulk manage

Select rows to enable, disable or delete them. Each action can succeed or fail independently. Check the response rather than assuming the entire selection changed; locked or in-use keywords may be refused.

Deleting a keyword is not the same as removing a published article from your website. Check the linked article separately before making changes to live content.

### Renaming a keyword

Rename an unused keyword inline, then check the response and any linked planned article. The current rename path can refresh the title, description and category of a planned article, but a successful rename does not guarantee that its separate plan refresh succeeded.

Renaming is blocked while related content is generating, ready for review, approved, scheduled or published. Follow the message for the article's actual state. You may need to wait for generation, discard a draft, remove approval or cancel a schedule; a live article's keyword cannot be changed through this action.

Keep the existing content if it still serves readers. Re-adding a keyword does not erase published material or establish that another article is needed.
